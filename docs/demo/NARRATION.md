## The full walkthrough (13:08)

Video: [`air-carrier-full-walkthrough.mp4`](air-carrier-full-walkthrough.mp4) · captions: [`.srt`](air-carrier-full-walkthrough.srt), [`.vtt`](air-carrier-full-walkthrough.vtt)

### 0:00 The problem, and one solution in four layers

One ship. Four official costs. None of them agree. The aircraft carrier John F. Kennedy, CVN-79, has a 2019 budget estimate of 11.3 billion dollars, a cost cap in law of 11.4 billion, a cost limitation baseline of 12.9 billion, and a 2027 budget total of 13.4 billion. Each figure is correct for its own purpose. Today, no office can trace what a weapon system costs from the budget request, through the contracts, to the asset, in a form that a program office, a cost estimator, a budget analyst and an auditor will all accept.

Our answer is one solution in four layers. At the base, MCP A Creator builds a tested, governed MCP server from a question and a table. Above it, domain MCP servers give agents cited access to documents, records and registers. The agent orchestration environment is where people and agents work the questions. At the top, a financial-control layer decides what is true and who may act. No model is in that layer. The differentiator is AI assisted development, joined to deterministic financial control.

All of it runs on Databricks today, as seven apps deployed from the public GitHub repository with one command. Five of them are MCP servers, with 56 tools: MCP A Creator, the docs server, the analyst server, the ops server, and the One Hub. The other two apps are the front center, and a probe that calls every server every fifteen minutes. A sixth server, built by the Creator on an unrelated sales table, stays in the repository as proof that the Creator is not tied to ships.

### 1:46 The front center: the start page and the financial-control layer

This is the front center. The start page states the solution: four layers and seven capabilities. Each of the three GSA tracks is served by a different layer: dataset access, service read integration, and service write integration in a sandbox.

The financial-control layer shows how a figure earns its place in a brief. Code analyzes the records in exact decimals. Code reconciles each figure to its source. Code cites the document, page, table and cell. A gate routes the figure. Then a person in the owning role decides. A model may plan, explain and draft. A model can never add a figure, change an amount, or approve anything.

Below are the seven capabilities, with what each one solves, for whom, and where it stands today. MCP A Creator. The docs server, which reads 34 PDFs and 7,804 pages down to the page and the table. The analyst server, which works a record that is missing data. The ops server, which the Creator built. The One Hub server, which holds the gate. The probe. And the front center itself.

The same page shows how it was tested, and what each result does not show. An outside evaluator's questions scored 83 percent. A blind reviewer would rely on nine of ten answers. 7,743 of 7,746 budget tie-outs pass, and the probe got 1,234 of 1,234 known answers right. Security is listed by risk, with the control in code for each one.

### 3:25 Impact: from one hull to every weapon system

The Impact page makes the case for scale. One carrier, read to the cent. The same agents and servers for every weapon system. The portfolio is large and slow moving: more than 2.4 trillion dollars across the department's costliest weapon programs, and its cost cannot yet be audited. The working pattern is simple. You ask. Agents plan. MCP servers fetch. The gate checks. You decide.

Five users bring five different questions: a senior leader, a program office cost analyst, a budget analyst, an accountant or auditor, and any analyst with a table. Each question is traced through the stack, tool by tool. With MCP A Creator, a new program gets its own governed server without a developer. Each new program supplies data, not software.

The same servers already read the whole Ford class. Four hulls come from budget books that are already loaded, each with its cost cap. The page records what CVN-78 and CVN-79 teach the ships that follow, and the eight steps that take any program from the public record to governed tools.

The plan runs program by program, in phases: the Ford class, Navy shipbuilding, the F-35, and then the 59 programs that have a 2027 selected acquisition report. The page ends by saying what it does not show, and by listing its sources.

### 4:52 The Brief, the ship and the cost position

The Brief page answers one question: what can we say about CVN-79's cost today? You start from your own question. The page then shows what you can say, with its basis. A total ship estimate of 13.41 billion dollars. 17.7 percent above the statutory cap. 75.8 percent traced to a named contract. And a delivery 54 months later than first planned. It also lists what to hold back, and why, and which open questions still block the number.

The ship page gives the context: the second ship of the Gerald R. Ford class, built at HII Newport News Shipbuilding, with every milestone cited to its source.

Under Cost, the Position page answers which figure to quote, and for what. The four published figures are four different kinds of number: a dated estimate, a limit in law, an adjusted limit, and a budget request. The bridge from the cap to the budget closes exactly, step by step. The budget total sits 2.01 billion dollars above the statutory cap, and each step names the mechanism behind it. What cannot yet be explained is stated as an open question, with the office that can close it.

The reconciliation ladder shows how far down the records go, from appropriation to disbursement. Cost composition shows what a named contract or vendor line accounts for. 75.8 percent of the hull is tied to a named record. The remaining 24.2 percent is a visibility gap, not a budget gap. The estimate history shows how the estimate moved across twelve budget submissions, and the statutory cap history sits beside it.

### 6:36 Agentic workflow, live: a specialist run and role-based approval

Budget execution is computed live from 1,875 prime contract transactions and 2,990 sub-awards. Now a live specialist run. We ask the Account Analyst to explain why one File C period does not tie to the transactions. Every step is labeled as code or as model. Code routes the task. Code packs the records in exact decimals. One model explains them.

Then code checks the answer again. The canvas re-check passes ten of ten conditions. A second model family, the Records Checker, confirms that every stated fact matches a record. Two proposals are ready for a person. Nothing is saved until a person applies it.

In review, the first proposal belongs to the Navy's Office of Financial Operations. We are signed in as program office PMS 379, so the system refuses, and says why. The role check runs in Unity Catalog, under the user's own sign-in. No role travels in a page or a cookie. The second proposal belongs to our own office, so we can apply it.

Back on the page, the obligation curve shows the handoff between the two prime contracts, and the largest contract actions are listed with their descriptions.

### 7:55 Audit: gaps, variances, asset value, the industrial base

Under Audit, the gap register answers which open questions block the number, and who can close them. 65 questions are registered. 59 are open, and 14 can be worked now with no new access.

A second specialist, the Gap Investigator, works one gap: when is construction in progress relieved for this hull? It reads the collection requirements, names the office that holds the answer, and marks what it could not confirm as blank, instead of guessing. The same checks pass, and one request is ready for a person to review.

The variance register keeps every place where public sources disagree. Conflicting values are retained with their provenance, and never resolved by picking one. One example: a December 2025 baseline of 13.529 billion dollars is printed, but not yet confirmed. The gate holds it for human review.

Asset valuation looks at the hull as the Navy's books see it: construction in progress, not yet a vessel. The page gives three structural reasons why capitalized value will never equal program cost: scope, timing and boundary.

The industrial base page shows who builds what. The shipyard is the hull builder and the systems integrator, but not the maker of most major systems. Tier two comes from 2,990 reported sub-awards: 426.8 million dollars, 261 subcontractors, in 35 states.

### 9:27 Agentic orchestration: Ask as an MCP host, the work queue, a reconciler run

Ask the evidence is an MCP host, not a chatbot. The agent lists the tools of three MCP servers, and sends each call to the server that owns it.

We ask two questions in plain language: why are there three different cost caps, and which File C periods fail to tie? Each answer comes back as a lead, with its citations, its tool calls, and the servers that answered. Where the tools cannot support a claim, the answer says so. An answer here is a starting point to verify, never a reported figure.

The work queue shows what needs a decision, what is blocked, and why. 16 decisions are waiting. Where no one holds the required role, the queue says so, and the item waits. It is never applied by default.

Brief readiness checks the claim ledger: 13 of 32 CVN-79 figures tie to source records today. The rest stay leads.

The Obligation Reconciler tests a contract's reported cost against its recorded obligations. We give it a figure to test: five million dollars. Code sums 927 contract actions in exact decimals, finds 4.58 billion dollars, and reports the difference to the cent. Each File C period is then compared with the transactions. Periods that tie are confirmed. Periods that do not are marked as a residual, with a button to explain each one. Again, two proposals wait for a person, and the run is recorded.

### 10:58 The record, the method, and the experts who hold the answers

The run log keeps every run, and every action by every person. The detail table holds the records behind the figures, with filters and export. The sources page says what each document family carries, and what it does not.

The method page explains how this is built, and why it can be challenged. It keeps what the method got wrong, on purpose. It also shows where AI sits. No AI output writes directly to a trusted table, and the gate decides in code.

The last page turns to the people who hold the answers. There are 59 open questions, and two sentences from the right person can close one. You pick a question in your area, and read the brief the system prepared. You say what you know, how you know it, and how sure you are. Your answer is recorded as a lead, with your name. It is never recorded as a certified figure.

### 11:50 How we know it works, security, close

How do we know it works? 1,770 tests pass from a clean clone, and 828 of them are hostile inputs. 421 hostile calls against the 30 read tools wrote nothing. On sealed questions, tools take two model families from guessing to answering: Claude from 14 percent correct to 88 percent, and GPT-5 mini from 1 percent to 79 percent. On an outside evaluator's questions, Claude scored 83 percent. Neither outside person is on the development team.

Controls are in code, not in prompts. Every app sits behind workspace sign-in, and runs as its own service principal. Inputs are validated, SQL parameters are bound, and retrieved text is treated as data. An outside scan on November 23rd found that an unexpected error could carry internal text to a client. Every server now withholds it, and a test checks it.

The next program supplies data, not software. The Creator, the readers, the gate and the front center are reused as they are.

AI builds and explains. Code proves the number. The owning role decides. One ship, reconciled to the cent, and ready for the next program.
